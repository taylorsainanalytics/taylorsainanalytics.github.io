FEATURED CASE STUDY

Building a Productivity Intelligence Framework

Measuring productivity across teams that all define success differently.

I partnered with executive leadership to develop a standardized analytics framework that transformed diverse operational metrics into one executive-ready productivity index.

FocusPerformance Measurement
ToolsTableau · Smartsheet

01 · THE BUSINESS PROBLEM

A simple question with no simple answer.

Leadership wanted to answer a deceptively simple question:

Are we becoming more productive?

The challenge was that every operational team defined productivity differently. Some measured throughput. Others tracked cost savings, automation adoption, compliance, quality improvements, or risk reduction.

Without a common measurement framework, comparing performance across teams—or evaluating organization-wide progress—was nearly impossible. The goal was not to replace team-specific metrics, but to preserve their meaning while creating a reliable enterprise-level view.

03 · RESEARCH & DISCOVERY

Before measuring productivity, I had to define value.

Rather than asking teams only what they already tracked, I centered discovery on a more useful question: How does your team create value?

Those conversations revealed that productivity could not be represented by one universal KPI. I therefore organized metrics into four standardized pillars that could accommodate different kinds of work while maintaining a common leadership narrative.

01

Efficiency

Speed, throughput, volume, and effort reduction.

02

Financial Impact

Cost savings, cost avoidance, revenue protection, and financial value.

03

Scalability

Automation, adoption, reuse, standardization, and organizational leverage.

04

Quality & Risk

Quality improvement, compliance, defect reduction, and operational risk mitigation.

05 · THE HARDEST ANALYTICAL PROBLEM

Not every improvement moves upward.

Revenue increasing is positive. Cycle time decreasing is also positive. The same is true for defects, rework, cost, and risk.

To make unlike metrics comparable, I designed direction-adjusted calculations. Each metric was assigned a scoring direction so that improvement was interpreted according to business meaning before results were normalized.

Throughput ↑Positive movement
Automation ↑Positive movement
Cycle Time ↓Positive movement
Defects ↓Positive movement

10 · LESSON LEARNED

The dashboard was the interface. The framework was the product.

The most valuable insight from this project was that successful analytics begins with defining the business problem before defining the metrics.

An analyst's responsibility is not simply to visualize available data. It is to create a shared structure that makes different work understandable and comparable without stripping away its meaning.
